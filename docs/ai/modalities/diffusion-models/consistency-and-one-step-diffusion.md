## Summary

Classic diffusion sampling is slow because it requires many reverse steps. Consistency models and shortcut-style models try to learn a function that maps noisy states to clean data consistently across time, enabling few-step or even one-step generation.

The central objective is:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t) \approx x_0
$$

for many noise levels $t$, with consistency constraints tying predictions across the trajectory.

## Concepts

- **Consistency model:** model whose outputs are consistent along a diffusion trajectory.
- **One-step generation:** sample directly from noise to data with one model evaluation.
- **Teacher trajectory:** path generated by a pretrained diffusion model or solver.
- **Shortcut model:** model conditioned on step size so it can jump over multiple denoising steps.
- **Self-consistency:** predictions from different points on the same trajectory agree.

## 1. Consistency condition

Suppose $x_t$ and $x_s$ lie on the same probability-flow trajectory and both correspond to the same clean sample $x_0$.

A consistency model wants:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t)
=
f_\theta(x_s,s)
$$

and ideally:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t)
=
x_0
$$

So the model learns a map from any point on the trajectory back to the same clean endpoint.

## 2. Consistency training

A typical training pair uses two neighboring trajectory points:

$$
x_t,\quad x_{t-\Delta t}
$$

and minimizes:

$$
\left\|
f_\theta(x_t,t)
-
f_{\theta^-}(x_{t-\Delta t},t-\Delta t)
\right\|^2
$$

where $\theta^-$ may be an EMA or target network.

This says:

> if two noisy states are on the same denoising path, their predicted clean outputs should match.

## 3. Why this enables one-step generation

If:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t) \approx x_0
$$

for every $t$, then generation can start from:

$$
x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)
$$

and output:

$$
\hat x_0 = f_\theta(x_T,T)
$$

in one step.

In practice, one-step quality is hard, so few-step sampling is often a better tradeoff.

## 4. Shortcut models

Shortcut models condition the network on a step size:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t,\Delta t)
$$

The model learns to move along the denoising trajectory by different jump lengths.

The training objective encourages compositional consistency:

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t,\Delta_1+\Delta_2)
\approx
f_\theta
\left(
f_\theta(x_t,t,\Delta_1),
t-\Delta_1,
\Delta_2
\right)
$$

The idea:

- small steps teach accurate local movement
- large steps teach shortcut generation
- consistency ties them together

This is attractive because it can train direct long jumps without a separate expensive teacher-sampling pipeline.

## 5. Why this is hard

One-step generation compresses a whole trajectory into one function evaluation.

The model must learn:

- global structure
- local detail
- class/condition alignment
- denoising direction
- correct data distribution

all from a very noisy input.

So the failure mode is:

$$
\text{fast sampling} \quad \text{but} \quad \text{lower sample quality}
$$

Consistency and shortcut methods are attempts to reduce this quality gap.

## 6. Practical view

For inference:

- DDPM: many steps, high quality, slow
- DDIM/ODE solvers: fewer steps, still iterative
- consistency models: few-step or one-step
- shortcut models: train step-size-conditioned jumps

The key engineering question is:

> how much sample quality are you willing to trade for fewer model evaluations?
