## Summary

LLM reinforcement learning appears information-theoretically inefficient. Next-token training observes a target at every token, while a long RL rollout may receive only one binary success signal. Autoregressive rollouts are also more expensive to generate than teacher-forced sequences.

Yet a small number of RL updates can produce large improvements on a narrow task. The apparent contradiction is resolved by separating three quantities:

1. **external supervision bandwidth:** how much new information the labels or rewards provide;
2. **objective alignment:** whether an update optimizes the behavior actually being evaluated;
3. **gradient-estimator quality:** the variance, bias, and credit-assignment quality of the update.

Pretraining has high bandwidth and broad coverage. Outcome-based RL has low bandwidth and poor token-level credit assignment, but its sequence-level objective can be closely aligned with task success. It works because pretraining and mid-training have already supplied most of the required knowledge and behavioral support. RL then uses sparse feedback to select and amplify successful trajectories rather than learning the entire capability from scratch.

This is a useful framework, not a proven general theory. In particular, a binary terminal reward is task-aligned but not “all signal”: it does not identify which actions caused the outcome.

## Concepts

- **Supervision bandwidth:** the amount of external information supplied by targets, rewards, teachers, or environments.
- **Objective alignment:** how closely the training objective matches the outcome ultimately desired.
- **Credit assignment:** determining which actions in a trajectory caused its reward.
- **Practical support:** trajectories the current policy can sample with non-negligible probability, not merely nonzero mathematical probability.
- **Reward entropy:** the uncertainty in observed rewards; an almost-always-zero reward supplies little information.
- **On-policy data:** trajectories generated by the current or very recent policy.
- **Staleness:** mismatch between the policy that generated a rollout and the policy being updated.
- **Mid-training:** dense training used to establish knowledge, formats, tool use, or behaviors before sparse-reward RL.

## 1. The bits-per-rollout paradox

Next-token pretraining minimizes

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NTP}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[
-\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\log\pi_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})
\right].
$$

Every observed token supplies a supervised target. If the vocabulary has size $V$, the information in one token is at most $\log_2V$ bits, with the actual surprisal under a predictive distribution given by $-\log_2p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$.

Outcome-based RL instead optimizes

$$
J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P},\,\tau\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}
[R(x,\tau)].
$$

If $R\in\{0,1\}$, the reward observation contains at most one bit. For success probability $p$, its entropy is

$$
H(R)=h_2(p)
=
-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)
\leq1.
$$

When success is extremely rare or almost guaranteed, $h_2(p)$ is much smaller than one bit. A rollout can also contain thousands of tokens generated through slow autoregressive decoding. On a simple external-labels-per-compute accounting, RL is dramatically less efficient than teacher-forced token prediction.

This conclusion is real but incomplete. It measures the amount of external supervision, not how well the induced update aligns with the desired downstream behavior.

## 2. Raw information and useful information are different

Pretraining uses a broad objective:

> Predict the distribution of natural text across a very large mixture of implicit tasks.

Outcome-based RL uses a narrow objective:

> Increase the probability of trajectories that satisfy this reward.

The next-token objective provides enough information to learn language, facts, code, representations, and many latent strategies. Once those prerequisites exist, most token-prediction updates need not point toward improvement on one particular downstream verifier.

A useful stage-wise view is:

| Stage | External supervision | Breadth | Main role |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Pretraining | dense token targets | very broad | acquire knowledge, representations, and behavioral support |
| Mid-training / SFT | dense token targets | targeted | expose desired domains, formats, and trajectories |
| Outcome RL | sparse sequence reward | narrow | reallocate probability toward task success |

Therefore, RL need not transmit the complete solution through its reward bit. The pretrained policy, sampled trajectory, and model derivatives already contain rich structure. The reward selects how that existing structure should be reorganized.

This does not make the reward information-dense. It makes a small amount of information useful because the model begins close to a useful behavior in policy space.

## 3. What a policy-gradient update actually contains

For a sampled trajectory $\tau=(a_1,\ldots,a_T)$, REINFORCE gives

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}
\left[
(R-b)
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\nabla_\theta
\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\right],
$$

where $b$ is a baseline.

The environment supplies only the scalar $R$, but the update direction also uses:

- every sampled action;
- every state or prefix;
- the policy's token probabilities;
- the pretrained model's parameter derivatives;
- a baseline or peer-group comparison.

The reward acts as a coefficient on a structured, model-dependent score function. It does not communicate a reasoning strategy token by token.

This distinction explains how one reward can produce a high-dimensional parameter update without violating information theory: optimization can extract and select structure already inside the policy, but cannot infer arbitrary missing knowledge from one bit.

## 4. Task alignment does not solve credit assignment

A successful terminal reward says that the complete trajectory worked. It does not say which token decisions were necessary.

The same positive advantage may reinforce:

- the decisive reasoning step;
- irrelevant wording;
- unnecessary detours;
- an accidental formatting choice;
- lucky exploration.

Likewise, a failed rollout may contain many correct intermediate steps. Broadcasting one terminal advantage across every action creates high variance and weak temporal credit assignment.

The careful statement is:

> Outcome reward is aligned with task success at the trajectory level but sparse and ambiguous at the action level.

Process rewards can provide denser credit, but a learned process reward may introduce model bias, reward hacking, or incorrect judgments. More feedback is not automatically better when its semantics are less reliable.

## 5. Two meanings of signal-to-noise ratio

The motivating essay describes RL as having higher signal-to-noise ratio for the rewarded task. This intuition is useful only if two different meanings of “noise” are kept separate.

### Gradient-estimator SNR

For a stochastic gradient $g$, one possible definition is

$$
\operatorname{SNR}(g)
=
\frac{\lVert\mathbb{E}[g]\rVert_2^2}
{\mathbb{E}[\lVert g-\mathbb{E}[g]\rVert_2^2]}.
$$

Sparse terminal-reward policy gradients often have **poor** estimator SNR because reward and trajectory sampling produce high variance.

Larger batches reduce gradient-estimation variance. A lower learning rate does not itself make an individual gradient estimate less noisy; it reduces displacement per update and, with momentum, integrates more estimates per unit of parameter movement.

### Alignment with the downstream objective

A different question is whether an update direction helps the target reward. One can think informally about its projection onto the target gradient:

$$
\langle g_{\mathrm{train}},g_{\mathrm{target}}\rangle.
$$

Next-token gradients may have low average alignment with one narrow verifier after broad competence has already been learned. RL gradients can be more aligned with that verifier even while being much noisier statistical estimates.

Thus RL can be simultaneously:

- low bandwidth;
- high variance;
- poor at token-level credit assignment;
- strongly aligned with a narrow sequence-level objective.

Calling the reward “all signal” incorrectly collapses these dimensions.

## 6. Why the pretrained prior is essential

Sparse-reward RL is effective when the desired behavior lies within the policy's **practical support**.

Softmax technically gives almost every finite token sequence nonzero probability. That mathematical fact is irrelevant if a successful trajectory would require an astronomical number of samples. Practical support means the policy can generate useful partial or complete solutions at an affordable sampling rate.

If one rollout succeeds with probability $p$, the probability of observing at least one success among $K$ independent rollouts is

$$
P(\text{at least one success})
=
1-(1-p)^K.
$$

For small $p$,

$$
1-(1-p)^K\approx Kp.
$$

If $p$ is effectively zero, increasing policy-gradient sophistication does not create a learning signal. The system must first change the model, task distribution, tools, or sampling strategy.

Pretraining supplies:

- language and domain knowledge;
- broad world and code models;
- useful response primitives;
- a prior over plausible reasoning paths;
- the ability to interpret prompts, feedback, and tool results.

RL then searches and reweights within this rich prior. It can discover a trajectory that never appeared verbatim in training by recombining learned components, but it cannot cheaply learn language or an entire missing domain from terminal bits alone.

## 7. Why mid-training matters before RL

Mid-training and SFT have two related roles.

### Raise the initial success probability

Dense examples can teach response protocols, tool schemas, reasoning formats, and domain-specific actions. This moves tasks from an all-failure regime toward a mixed-outcome frontier where advantages become informative.

### Reduce the required policy change

RL generally operates under explicit or implicit constraints:

- limited updates;
- KL penalties;
- clipping;
- entropy preservation;
- narrow rollout budgets.

Mid-training places useful behavior near enough that RL can improve it without a destructive policy shift.

A practical pipeline is therefore:

```text
pretraining
-> targeted mid-training or SFT
-> verify nontrivial pass@k
-> on-policy RL near the learning frontier
-> distill or merge specialized policies if needed
```

This is not evidence that RL only memorizes behaviors already explicitly demonstrated. It says that the knowledge and action primitives needed for successful exploration must be reachable.

## 8. Why successful-trace SFT is not the same as RL

Suppose the current model generates trajectories and only successful ones are retained. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning minimizes

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RSFT}}
=
-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid R=1)}
\left[
\sum_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
\right].
$$

This is not ordinary imitation of an unrelated teacher, nor is its expected gradient necessarily zero. Conditioning on success changes the trajectory distribution and can improve the policy.

However, it differs from policy-gradient RL:

| Successful-trace SFT | Policy-gradient RL |
|---|---|
| retains positive trajectories | can use positive and negative advantages |
| usually gives all retained tokens positive weight | weights trajectories or tokens by relative outcome |
| discards information from failures | baselines compare successes and failures |
| may overconcentrate on a few successful modes | can preserve exploration through entropy/KL controls |
| becomes stale after policy updates | can regenerate from the current policy |

Iterative self-SFT can work, and RL does not universally dominate it. The comparison depends on refresh frequency, filtering, reward density, sequence length, diversity, and optimization. The important distinction is how each objective transforms outcome information into token updates—not merely the number of supervised tokens.

## 9. Dense on-policy distillation adds another axis

On-policy distillation samples trajectories from the student but receives token-level log-probability feedback from a teacher. It combines:

- relevance to states the student actually visits;
- much denser supervision than terminal reward;
- a teacher objective that may or may not match the external task reward.

This demonstrates why “more bits should always win” is insufficient. Dense teacher feedback is valuable when the teacher knows the desired behavior and its token preferences are relevant. It can waste capacity on incidental style or token choices when only final correctness matters.

RL remains useful for discovering policies under an external verifier. Once a good policy exists, distillation can often transfer it more efficiently than repeating search.

## 10. The verifier and curriculum contain information too

Counting only reward outputs omits information engineered into the surrounding system:

- prompt distribution;
- task generator;
- executable tests;
- compiler or theorem checker;
- tool API;
- environment dynamics;
- reward-model training data;
- curriculum and sampling policy.

A compiler may emit one success bit per run, but the mapping from programs to outcomes embodies a large reusable specification. That information is amortized across many rollouts.

The reward channel is therefore only one part of the learning system's information budget. A rigorous comparison must state what is being conditioned on and what external artifacts are counted.

This does not eliminate the sparse-feedback problem. Even a rich verifier may expose only pass/fail for one attempted trajectory, leaving the policy to discover which changes matter.

## 11. Why on-policy consistency matters

Let $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ generate an action and $\pi_\theta$ be the policy being updated. Importance sampling uses

$$
\rho_t
=
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
$$

For a full sequence, the likelihood ratio is a product:

$$
\rho_{1:T}
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}\rho_t,
$$

or in log space,

$$
\log\rho_{1:T}
=
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\rho_t.
$$

Tiny systematic per-token mismatches can therefore accumulate over long trajectories. Stale weights, different sampling kernels, precision differences, chat-template changes, or inconsistent masks may produce:

- extreme importance ratios;
- higher-variance corrections;
- pervasive clipping or token dropping;
- biased updates when corrections are truncated;
- divergence between reported and actual behavior policies.

This gives a direct reason for LLM RL's sensitivity to trainer-rollout mismatch. A “finely tuned loss valley” may be an additional intuition, but importance-ratio compounding and distribution shift already explain much of the engineering failure mode.

Off-policy methods do not remove the tradeoff. Exact correction can have extreme variance; clipping controls variance by reintroducing bias.

## 12. LLM RL still performs exploration

Pretraining dramatically reduces the exploration burden, but it does not remove exploration. The policy still searches over:

- reasoning decompositions;
- code edits;
- tool calls;
- recovery strategies;
- long-horizon action sequences.

The difference from much classical RL is that broad competence and many action priors have already been amortized into the model. LLM RL begins with an agent that understands language, tasks, and often tools rather than learning perception and action from scratch.

Claims that classical RL generally tolerates replay while LLM RL does not are too broad. Classical RL includes both on-policy and off-policy families, each with stability and correction constraints. The useful contrast is pretrained-policy refinement versus learning a policy and representations jointly from a much weaker prior.

## 13. What the framework explains

The framework predicts several recurring observations:

### Rapid but narrow gains

If competence is already present and only probability allocation is wrong, a small number of targeted updates can produce a large change on the rewarded distribution.

### Jagged capability profiles

RL supplies strong signal on rewarded tasks and little signal elsewhere. Improvements can therefore be sharp and domain-specific, with regressions outside the reward distribution.

### Cold starts

When successful behavior is outside practical support, sparse reward cannot bootstrap it efficiently. Dense training or curriculum changes are needed first.

### Sensitivity to reward quality

Narrow objective alignment is an advantage only when the reward matches the intended behavior. A precisely optimized proxy can produce reward hacking more efficiently than broad pretraining.

### Value of hybrid pipelines

Pretraining, mid-training, RL, and distillation solve different information and optimization problems. The strongest system is normally a staged pipeline rather than one universally superior objective.

## 14. What remains speculative

The following claims from the motivating argument should be treated as hypotheses rather than established results:

- that most pretraining gradients are noise for downstream reward in a measurable SNR sense;
- that learning-rate annealing and RL gains reflect the same loss-landscape mechanism;
- that LLM on-policy sensitivity primarily results from being “far down” a scaling curve;
- that iterative on-policy SFT is generally inferior to policy-gradient RL;
- that classical RL's parameter scaling limitations primarily follow from reward bit rate;
- that large-scale learning should always prefer low-bias, high-variance algorithms.

These ideas are plausible research directions, but they conflate objective mismatch, estimator variance, representation learning, exploration, and systems effects unless tested separately.

## 15. Practical implications

Before starting an expensive RL run:

1. measure the base policy's per-prompt and aggregate `pass@k`;
2. estimate reward entropy and within-group reward variance;
3. move all-failure tasks into range using SFT, mid-training, tools, curricula, or better sampling;
4. audit whether the reward matches the intended deployed behavior;
5. keep rollout and trainer policies numerically and semantically synchronized;
6. monitor importance ratios, clipping fraction, KL, entropy, response length, and staleness;
7. retain general-capability evaluations to detect narrow-objective regressions;
8. compare RL with rejection-sampling SFT and on-policy distillation at matched rollout and compute budgets;
9. use process feedback only when its additional density outweighs its bias and exploitability;
10. report learning progress per rollout token and accelerator-hour, not only optimizer steps.

The durable mental model is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{pretraining acquires broad structure}
\quad+\quad
\text{mid-training makes success reachable}
\quad+\quad
\text{RL targets outcome probability}
}
$$

Sparse reward works when it only needs to steer a competent policy. It is not an information-efficient replacement for learning that competence.
