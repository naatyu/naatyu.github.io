## Summary

Cross-entropy is not the only statistically valid objective for next-token prediction. It belongs to the larger family of **proper scoring rules**: losses whose expected value is minimized when the model reports the true conditional distribution.

The important distinctions are:

- properness determines whether truthful probabilities are optimal;
- the scoring rule determines how errors at different confidence levels are weighted;
- the output mapping determines the gradient geometry in logit space;
- a Fenchel–Young construction can derive a matched output mapping and loss from a generalized entropy.

Softmax plus cross-entropy is the matched pair generated by Shannon entropy. Other generalized entropies produce alternatives such as sparsemax or entmax. These alternatives are mathematically coherent, but there is not yet strong evidence that replacing cross-entropy improves large-language-model pretraining.

## 1. The target is a distribution, although training observes one token

For a context $x$, let:

- $p \in \Delta^V$ be the unknown true next-token distribution;
- $q \in \Delta^V$ be the model distribution;
- $Y \sim p$ be the single token observed in the dataset;
- $S(q,Y)$ be the loss assigned after observing $Y$.

Here $\Delta^V$ is the probability simplex over a vocabulary of size $V$.

Language-model data usually provides one sampled continuation rather than the complete vector $p$. Therefore, a loss estimable from individual samples has population risk

$$
L(p,q)
=
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}[S(q,Y)]
=
\sum_{i=1}^{V}p_i S(q,i).
$$

This risk is linear in the unknown target distribution $p$. That is not a special property of cross-entropy; it follows from only observing samples from $p$.

## 2. Proper scoring rules

A loss-oriented scoring rule is **proper** if reporting the true distribution is always optimal:

$$
p \in \underset{q\in\Delta^V}{\arg\min}\; L(p,q).
$$

It is **strictly proper** if the minimum is unique:

$$
\underset{q\in\Delta^V}{\arg\min}\; L(p,q)=\{p\}.
$$

Strict propriety gives a form of probability calibration at the population optimum: if the model family and optimization are sufficient, it has no incentive to report a distribution different from $p$.

This guarantee does **not** say that two proper losses have the same:

- gradients;
- optimization speed;
- robustness to corrupted labels;
- finite-data behavior;
- behavior under model misspecification;
- systems cost.

## 3. Proper losses come from concave generalized entropies

Define the Bayes risk, or generalized entropy, as

$$
H(p)=L(p,p).
$$

Because $L(p,q)$ is linear in $p$ and $H(p)$ is its pointwise minimum over $q$, $H$ is concave. For a differentiable concave $H$, its tangent at $q$ gives

$$
L(p,q)
=
H(q)+(p-q)^\top \nabla H(q).
$$

Since $p$ enters linearly, the loss for observing class $i$ is

$$
S(q,i)
=
H(q)+(e_i-q)^\top\nabla H(q),
$$

where $e_i$ is the one-hot vector for class $i$.

For non-differentiable entropies, a subgradient replaces $\nabla H(q)$. Strict concavity generally induces strict propriety; weaker concavity can permit multiple optimal reports.

### Logarithmic score

Shannon entropy is

$$
H_{\mathrm{Shannon}}(p)=-\sum_j p_j\log p_j.
$$

Its scoring rule, up to an irrelevant additive constant, is

$$
S_{\log}(q,i)=-\log q_i.
$$

For a one-hot next-token target, this is cross-entropy.

### Brier score

The Brier loss is

$$
S_{\mathrm{Brier}}(q,i)
=
\lVert q-e_i\rVert_2^2
=
\sum_j q_j^2-2q_i+1.
$$

Its generalized entropy is

$$
H_{\mathrm{Brier}}(p)=1-\sum_j p_j^2.
$$

Both log loss and Brier loss are strictly proper, yet their training dynamics can be very different.

### Other families

Tsallis, spherical, and some Rényi-related constructions also produce proper scoring rules. “Proper” should be checked for the exact parameter range and sign convention: a named entropy family does not make every parameterization strictly proper.

## 4. Why the logarithmic score is special

Suppose the per-example loss is **local**: after observing class $i$, it depends only on the probability assigned to that class,

$$
S(q,i)=f(q_i).
$$

Under standard differentiability and regularity assumptions, strict propriety forces

$$
f(q_i)=-c\log q_i + C,
\qquad c>0.
$$

Thus the logarithmic score is essentially the unique strictly proper local scoring rule on a finite outcome space. Brier loss is non-local because it also examines probabilities assigned to every incorrect class.

This helps explain the practical dominance of cross-entropy: one only needs the target token's log-probability, and the result has a direct coding interpretation.

## 5. The loss in probability space is only half of the optimization problem

Let $z\in\mathbb{R}^V$ be logits and $q=\operatorname{softmax}(z)$. For softmax cross-entropy,

$$
\nabla_z S_{\log}(q,i)=q-e_i.
$$

This simple residual gradient is bounded and does not acquire an additional softmax Jacobian factor.

For Brier loss composed with softmax,

$$
\nabla_z S_{\mathrm{Brier}}(q,i)
=
2\left(\operatorname{diag}(q)-qq^\top\right)(q-e_i).
$$

The matrix in front is the softmax Jacobian. If the model is extremely confident in the wrong class, softmax is near a simplex vertex and this Jacobian becomes small. Consequently, Brier-plus-softmax can produce a weak corrective gradient exactly when a confident error must be repaired.

This leads to an important rule:

> Do not compare probability-space losses without also comparing the output mapping and the resulting logit-space gradients.

## 6. Fenchel–Young losses derive matched output/loss pairs

Start with a concave generalized entropy $H(q)$ and define

$$
\Phi(z)
=
\max_{q\in\Delta^V}
\left\{q^\top z+H(q)\right\}.
$$

The matched probability mapping is

$$
q(z)
=
\underset{q\in\Delta^V}{\arg\max}
\left\{q^\top z+H(q)\right\}.
$$

When the solution is unique, $q(z)=\nabla\Phi(z)$. If $H(e_i)=0$, the corresponding Fenchel–Young loss for target $i$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FY}}(z,i)=\Phi(z)-z_i.
$$

Its gradient is again the clean residual

$$
\nabla_z\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FY}}(z,i)=q(z)-e_i.
$$

This construction avoids arbitrarily combining a probability-space loss with softmax. The entropy, output transformation, and convex logit-space loss are derived together.

### Important matched pairs

| Generalized entropy | Output mapping | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Shannon | softmax | dense probabilities; standard cross-entropy |
| quadratic / Gini-type | sparsemax | Euclidean projection onto the simplex; exact zeros |
| Tsallis $\alpha$ family | $\alpha$-entmax | continuum between softmax-like and sparse outputs |

For the common entmax convention:

- $\alpha\rightarrow 1$ recovers softmax;
- $\alpha=2$ gives sparsemax;
- $\alpha=1.5$ gives the commonly used entmax-1.5 transformation.

Exact constants vary across entropy normalizations, so an implementation's definition must be checked before comparing loss values or hyperparameters.

## 7. What sparsity changes

Softmax assigns strictly positive probability to every token. Sparsemax and entmax can assign exact zero probability to many tokens.

Potential benefits include:

- a more selective distribution;
- reduced influence from clearly implausible classes;
- interpretable support sets;
- possible robustness when labels are genuinely noisy.

Potential problems for language models include:

- rare tokens are often meaningful rather than annotation noise;
- zero probability implies infinite logarithmic loss if another component later takes $\log q_i$;
- KL-based distillation and regularization need careful support handling;
- sampling kernels and fused cross-entropy implementations are optimized for softmax;
- solving a vocabulary-wide threshold can be expensive;
- standard perplexity is tied to log loss and is not directly comparable to an alternative training loss.

Sparse outputs therefore change more than the loss function. They affect training kernels, evaluation, distillation, generation, and serving assumptions.

## 8. A cheap Brier implementation in PyTorch

Materializing a one-hot tensor with shape `[batch, sequence, vocabulary]` is unnecessary. The identity

$$
\lVert q-e_i\rVert_2^2=\sum_j q_j^2-2q_i+1
$$

gives:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F


def brier_from_logits(logits: torch.Tensor,
                      targets: torch.Tensor,
                      reduction: str = "mean") -> torch.Tensor:
    """Multi-class Brier loss without constructing one-hot targets."""
    probs = logits.softmax(dim=-1)
    target_probs = probs.gather(
        dim=-1,
        index=targets.unsqueeze(-1),
    ).squeeze(-1)

    per_token = probs.square().sum(dim=-1) - 2.0 * target_probs + 1.0

    if reduction == "none":
        return per_token
    if reduction == "sum":
        return per_token.sum()
    if reduction == "mean":
        return per_token.mean()
    raise ValueError(f"Unknown reduction: {reduction}")
```

For causal-LM batches, shift the predictions and replace ignored labels with a valid temporary index before `gather`; masking only after `gather` would still make an `ignore_index=-100` target fail:

```python
shift_logits = logits[..., :-1, :].contiguous()
shift_labels = labels[..., 1:].contiguous()

valid = shift_labels.ne(-100)
safe_labels = shift_labels.masked_fill(~valid, 0)
per_token = brier_from_logits(shift_logits, safe_labels, reduction="none")
loss = per_token[valid].mean()
```

This implementation avoids the one-hot target but not the full softmax or the sum over the vocabulary. It also retains the softmax-Jacobian gradient issue described above.

## 9. Research directions, not established recipes

One hypothesis proposed in the motivating blog is to use cross-entropy early, when strong correction of confident mistakes matters, and introduce Brier loss late, when suppressing the effect of suspected noisy samples might help.

A simple interpolation would be

$$
S_\lambda
=
(1-\lambda)S_{\log}
+
\lambda S_{\mathrm{Brier}},
\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
$$

A non-negative mixture of proper scores remains proper, and it is strictly proper while a strictly proper component has positive weight. However, this does **not** establish that the schedule is useful for LLMs. The scales of the two losses differ, and changing $\lambda$ changes the optimization geometry during training.

A credible experiment should compare:

1. a cross-entropy baseline at equal tokens and compute;
2. Brier plus softmax, to isolate the proposed robustness behavior;
3. a matched Fenchel–Young alternative such as entmax;
4. fixed mixtures and late-stage schedules;
5. clean validation log loss, calibration, rare-token accuracy, and downstream tasks;
6. deliberately corrupted data separately from naturally ambiguous language;
7. throughput, memory, and numerical stability at the real vocabulary size.

The blog derives the alternatives but does not report large-language-model experiments demonstrating a gain. The late-stage Brier proposal should therefore be treated as an unverified research direction.

## 10. Practical takeaways

- Cross-entropy is one member of a broad class of strictly proper objectives.
- Its special advantages are locality, coding meaning, efficient kernels, and the simple softmax-logit gradient $q-e_i$.
- Properness alone is not enough to predict trainability or downstream quality.
- Brier-plus-softmax may suppress gradients on confidently wrong predictions.
- Fenchel–Young duality is the principled way to derive a compatible output mapping and loss from a generalized entropy.
- Sparsemax and entmax are coherent alternatives, but exact zeros create substantial LLM-specific complications.
- There is no evidence in the source that replacing cross-entropy is already a generally better LLM-pretraining recipe.
